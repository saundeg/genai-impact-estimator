Generative AI Environmental Impact Estimator

Estimates the annual inference electricity, carbon and water associated with generative AI provided to staff and students under an institutional licence. Every input below is colour-coded by how well it is evidenced, because the reliability of the answer is set almost entirely by the weakest input — not the strongest.

00 Known applications Measured

If a supplier or an internal system already tells you exactly how many queries an AI application handled — as many chatbot and assistant platforms do — enter it directly here rather than estimating it. Each row shows its electricity, carbon and water on its own, using the conversion factors set in section 04 below. Each measured query is then subtracted from the modelled interaction count before the scenario estimate is calculated, because the weekly frequency in section 02 is defined as covering all of your licensed tools — so a measured query is already inside it. Versions 2 and 3 of this tool added measured energy on top without deducting anything, and counted those queries twice; the claim in the v2 and v3 notes that nothing was double-counted was not correct.

02 How much they use it

Pick a starting scenario, then adjust. Note that adoption is now evidenced — what is genuinely uncertain is how much of that use runs through your licences rather than personal free accounts.

Students

HEPI 2026: 95% of UK undergraduates report using AI in at least one way. Note that this is an ever-used measure. Multiplying it by a weekly frequency treats every occasional user as a weekly user, which overstates the total. Use the low bound if you want to approximate weekly-active students instead.

The single biggest uncertainty in this model. HEPI cannot tell paid institutional ChatGPT from free personal accounts. The bounds are the values used by the Cautious and Intensive scenario presets, so they are not new inventions.

No published UK figure exists for prompt frequency. Enter the mean across all licensed users, not the typical user: a minority of heavy users pulls the mean well above the median, so an intuitively typical value will understate the total.

Staff

Weak evidence. US working-age adoption for work was 36–45% in 2024–25; information and technology sectors reached 56–76%. The low bound reflects the general working-age figure rather than the technology-sector one.

03 What kind of work they do

This is the change that matters most against a single-figure model. A long reasoning query uses roughly thirteen times the energy of a short one, and one agentic task can trigger many model calls behind a single click. Shares must total 100%.

One user action in a coding or research agent can fire many separate model calls. Energy per agentic task is this number multiplied by the long-reasoning figure.

Video generation is measured at roughly 944 Wh per five-second clip — about three hundred times a long reasoning query, and seven hundred times an image. Even a fraction of a percent of video use will dominate your total. Leave the video share at zero unless you know it happens, and if you do set it, check what it does to the blended figure.

Short text query Published

Oviedo et al., Joule (2026): median 0.31 Wh, interquartile range 0.16–0.60, for a frontier-scale model (>200B parameters) at production scale. Cross-checks against Google's metered median Gemini prompt of 0.24 Wh.

Long reasoning query Published

Oviedo et al.: at roughly 5,000 output tokens (15× a typical query), the median rises 13× to 3.91 Wh, interquartile range 2.15–7.05.

Image generation Published High bound constructed

Luccioni, Jernite and Strubell (ACM FAccT, 2024) measured 2.907 Wh per image across 88 models. A 2025 study of a lighter model at 512×512 measured about 0.3 Wh, roughly ten times less — the gap is driven by parameter count, resolution and denoising steps. The central value takes the higher, better-evidenced figure; the high bound carries the same order-of-magnitude spread upward, since the FAccT study reports the largest models well above the mean.

Video generation Central measured Bounds constructed

Measurements on CogVideoX found a five-second clip required about 3.4 megajoules of GPU energy, or roughly 944 Wh — around seven hundred times a high-quality image. This is a single unreplicated laboratory measurement on one open model. The bounds are deliberately wide — half to three times the central value — to reflect that, rather than the zero-width range a single study would otherwise imply. They are not published bounds. Scale up for longer clips.

Image and video figures are laboratory measurements on open models, not metered production figures from the services your institution licenses, so they are less directly comparable than the text figures above.

Uncertainty ranges are recomputed from these fields, from the task mix, and — new in this version — from the low and high bounds on licence share and interaction frequency in section 02. Those two were previously excluded from the range despite being the dominant unknowns, which made the reported band far narrower than the stated order-of-magnitude accuracy. The band is now an envelope with every assumption at its bound simultaneously, so it is wide by construction and the true value sits well inside it.

04 Carbon and water conversion

Electricity is only the intermediate step. These factors turn kilowatt-hours into the two things a sustainability report actually needs.

Carbon

Grid emissions factor, kgCO₂e/kWh Published

DESNZ 2026 UK electricity generation factor: 0.13096, down from 0.17700 in 2025. This is the location-based factor — the average carbon content of grid electricity. Transmission and distribution losses are now a separate field alongside this one, and are added to it.

Market-based emissions factor, kgCO₂e/kWh Derived

Google’s 0.03 gCO₂e per median text prompt is not a single electricity figure. It is 0.023 g of Scope 2 market-based electricity plus 0.010 g of Scope 1 and 3, the latter being embodied hardware for the accelerators, host CPU and DRAM. Only the electricity component belongs in a factor that gets multiplied by kilowatt-hours: 0.023 g ÷ 0.00024 kWh = 95.8 gCO₂e/kWh, or 0.0958 kgCO₂e/kWh. The embodied 0.010 g is deliberately not carried, because hardware manufacture is outside this tool’s stated boundary and embodied emissions do not scale with electricity in any case. Bounds carry the published per-prompt spread through the same derivation.

Transmission and distribution losses, kgCO₂e/kWh Published

DESNZ 2026 (published 11 June 2026) gives transmission and distribution losses as 0.01299 kgCO₂e/kWh, down from 0.01853 in the 2025 set — a fall of about 30%, steeper than generation itself. Added to the 2026 generation factor of 0.13096 this gives 0.14395 for electricity consumed including losses. Note that these losses belong in Scope 3 category 3, not Scope 2, so they are reported separately from the generation figure. Set to zero if your framework requires generation only. Bounds span the 2025 and 2026 published values, since which vintage applies depends on the reporting year.

Facility overhead (PUE) Already included

No overhead multiplier is applied, and none should be, because overhead is already inside both energy sources. Google decomposes its 0.24 Wh median text prompt as 0.14 Wh active accelerators, 0.06 Wh host CPU and DRAM, 0.02 Wh idle and reserved capacity, and 0.02 Wh data-centre overhead including PUE. Oviedo et al. state that their framework estimates inference energy from token throughput, node power and overhead under large-scale deployment assumptions. Applying a further multiplier would count overhead twice. This was recorded as an open question in v3; it is now closed against the primary sources.

Water

Direct cooling water, L/kWh Measured

Water Usage Effectiveness, ISO/IEC 30134-9. Published fleet figures: AWS 0.12 (2025), Microsoft 0.27 (FY2025), industry average about 0.36 per Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Google roughly 1.1 implied by its 0.26 mL per 0.24 Wh prompt. Microsoft's per-query range starts at 0.0 because some sites are closed-loop or air-cooled.

Indirect water from power generation, L/kWh Published

Water consumed upstream generating the electricity. Often 80% or more of the true total. Published values: about 1.8 for a generic European grid, 3.92 disclosed by Meta for purchased electricity, 7.6 estimated by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. Set to zero if you want direct cooling water only.

Read the small result carefully. This model will almost certainly tell you that AI inference is a negligible share of your footprint. That is a true statement about inference within this boundary, and not a finding that AI is environmentally insignificant. Excluded from the boundary: training the models, manufacturing the hardware, constructing and operating the wider data-centre estate, and the electricity your users' own laptops draw. Embedded supply-chain water alone exceeds 99% of the corporate water footprint at one major operator. Do not let this tool be quoted as an all-in figure.

    What that means in everyday terms

    Raw kilowatt-hours mean little to most readers. These comparisons put the figures on a human scale. The basis for each is shown so you can check it — or swap it for one you prefer.

      Handle with care. Equivalences are illustrative, never evidence. The denominator you pick controls the impression: the same figure can be made to sound trivial or alarming depending on whether you compare it to boiling a kettle or to powering homes. Quote the basis alongside the comparison, and never substitute an equivalence for the actual number.

      Please note. This is illustrative scenario modelling of institutionally licensed generative AI inference, pending supplier usage data. Interaction frequency and licence share are planning assumptions and remain the dominant source of uncertainty. Two known biases remain and are not corrected here. Downward: all compute is costed at the UK grid factor, although inference for UK tenants is served substantially from Ireland, the Netherlands and the United States, where grid carbon is materially higher. Upward: adoption is an ever-use measure multiplied by a weekly frequency, which counts occasional users as weekly ones. Facility overhead is not a bias — it is already inside both energy sources, so no multiplier is applied and none is needed. Ask your suppliers which regions serve your tenant, and for tenant-level query volumes — that would improve this estimate more than any further refinement of the model.
